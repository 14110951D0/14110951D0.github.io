PhD, ETH Zürich · Spinal Cord Artificial Intelligence (SCAI) Lab & Sensory-Motor Systems Lab

I build the parts of an explainable clinical digital twin: models of individual patients that keep their state in a form a clinician can inspect, predict how it changes, and answer what-if questions about treatment. My work joins two causal traditions that are usually kept apart — structure learning with graphical models, and potential-outcomes reasoning — with deep temporal models that are interpretable by design rather than explained after the fact.

I completed my doctorate at ETH Zürich with Dr. Diego Paez-Granados, Prof. Robert Riener, and Prof. Julia Vogt, working closely with clinicians at the Swiss Paraplegic Centre. I am now in Prof. Christopher Yau's group at the University of Oxford, working on simulation-based pre-training for robust and trustworthy clinical prediction models.

What I work on

Research

A clinical record tells you what happened — never what would have happened. My research builds models that clinicians can question anyway: graphs that say which factors carry risk, temporal models whose explanation is the model itself, and simulation frameworks that manufacture the ground truth needed to check a causal claim.

HAPI

Risk flagged upstream — with a graph you can argue with

After spinal cord injury, a pressure injury becomes visible only once the damage is done, and today's risk forms give a score with no reason. Mixed-variable graphical modelling learns a causal graph of the risk factors from 250 patients, with clinicians' timeline orienting the arrows, and predicts from that graph. The readable graph costs nothing in accuracy; its edges are hypotheses to argue with, not facts.

  • AUROC 0.952 with the full Markov blanket (nested CV)
  • Admission-time features alone: balanced accuracy 0.925 (held-out split)

PI Fusion

Two small cohorts — share structure, never parameters

Surgeons want to know what drives complications after a pressure injury is repaired, yet each cohort (250 acute-care patients; 266 surgical episodes from 174 patients) is too small to learn a reliable graph alone. Causal graph fusion shares structure, never parameters: a graph over the shared variables constrains each cohort's graph and steers a frozen tabular foundation model. A compact graph-selected feature set predicts complications about as well as all 75 features.

  • Age, albumin, haemoglobin → length of stay in both cohorts
  • Compact graph set ≈ all 75 features (held-out AUROC)
  • Graph F1 0.62 → 0.70 against synthetic known truth

Wound microbiome

Same care, opposite outcomes — does the wound's community explain why?

Two pressure wounds can match in stage, size, site and care, yet months later one has improved and the other has stalled or worsened. Three analytically distinct methods, applied to 16S rRNA profiles of 55 wounds, test whether the bacterial community explains healing. All three agree: anaerobic genera are enriched in wounds that fail to improve, and a burden score over seven genera discriminates about as well as the full classifier, pending prospective validation.

  • Anaerobic burden ≈2× in poorly healing wounds, p = 0.002
  • Anaerobes are the network hubs (top hub degree 0.87)
  • 7-genus burden score ≈ gradient-boosted classifier

Markerless gait

Few clinics have motion capture — gait biomechanics from a single video

Gait analysis guides rehabilitation after spinal cord injury, but it relies on motion capture and force plates that few clinics have. On the SCAI SCI Gait dataset (239 adults), a parametric body mesh fitted to a single side-view video drives a scaled OpenSim model. Markerless kinematics agreed with motion capture, predicted ground-reaction forces closely matched force plates, and Markov-blanket graph analysis found waveform components conditionally associated with functional independence.

  • Kinematics vs motion capture: r = 0.68–0.90
  • Ground-reaction forces vs force plates: r = 0.85–0.87
  • SCAI SCI Gait dataset: 239 adults with SCI

GARLIC

ICU records are irregular — a forecast that shows its own reasons

Intensive-care records are irregular and up to 98 percent empty, and an explanation added afterwards need not be faithful. GARLIC treats gaps as information and learns a time-lagged dependency graph between signals; its attention weights and edges are themselves the explanation. It ranks first on three ICU benchmarks against 15 baselines, and its explanations pass a prespecified remove-and-retrain audit.

  • Best AUROC and AUPRC on 3 ICU benchmarks, 15 baselines
  • MIMIC-III AUROC 90.1 vs 89.2 for the runner-up
  • Remove-and-retrain audit: 8/8 checks vs 5/8 (RETAIN)

KarmaTS

No ground truth for causal claims — so we built one

Causal claims on real data have no ground truth: known-truth benchmarks use random graphs, and real recordings hide the mechanism. KarmaTS makes a discrete-time causal process executable: starting from a real recording, an algorithm proposes the structure, an expert can edit it, and the result can be run forward, intervened on or shifted. Six discovery algorithms were graded against template graphs with a known answer; expert-elicited graphs and a user study are still to come.

  • 6 discovery algorithms graded on F1 and SID
  • Varies series length, lag, density and hidden variables
  • Its known-truth logic carries into ICare-DT's counterfactual benchmark

ICare-DT

What would an action do? Effects graded, personal updates checked

Only one future is recorded, an average effect is not one patient's, and refitting on a few hours of one patient risks harmful drift. ICare-DT learns one representation of an ICU patient's history, with separately testable heads for risk, trajectory, treatment effect and gated personal updates. On semi-synthetic known truth it cut effect error over tenfold versus naive association; a personal update failed its prespecified check and was refused.

  • Treatment-effect error 0.008 vs 0.155 naive
  • Error 0.854 for RMSN, 0.929 for causal transformer
  • Gate vetoed update: 0 / 48 subgroup tests passed

BioBO

Vast search space, tiny budget — prior biology must guide without deciding

A lab can afford only a few hundred single-gene experiments, under 5% of some 10,000 candidate genes, so prior biology must guide the search without deciding it. BioBO is a Bayesian optimisation loop whose surrogate fuses several data modalities and whose acquisition uses a biology-informed prior that fades as real data arrive. On GeneDisco CRISPR screens it finds the best genes sooner than standard Bayesian optimisation; a deliberately wrong prior falls back to standard performance.

  • 4–40% labelling effort saved by modality fusion
  • Biology prior improves the search in 23 of 24 settings
  • Wrong prior: 0.091 vs 0.093 for standard BO (IFN-γ)

Now at Oxford

Simulation-based pre-training for robust clinical prediction

I have joined Prof. Christopher Yau's group in the Nuffield Department of Women's & Reproductive Health and the Big Data Institute, on an MRC–NIHR funded project developing a simulation-based pre-training framework for robust, trustworthy clinical prediction models, with exemplar studies in ovarian cancer prognosis, COVID-19 outcomes and childhood mental health. I bring two threads of my PhD to it — causal simulation in the spirit of KarmaTS, and BioBO's principle of injecting domain knowledge as a regularising prior — and approach it in three steps:

  1. SimulateChallenging scenarios and constraints not seen in the historical data, such as data drift and algorithmic fairness.
  2. Pre-trainThe model learns from simulated data, with domain knowledge as an informed prior.
  3. Fine-tuneReal clinical data adapts the model while the prior regularises against biased records.
  • Resilience to data drift and distribution shift
  • Fairness across patient subgroups
  • Trustworthy, explainable predictions
